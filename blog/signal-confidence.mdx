I keep a mental list of things that are technically correct and still misleading, and "a number with no error bar" has been near the top of it for a while. `file_risk_score` is a good example from my own tool: it's the same kind of number whether I'm pointing it at a huge, disciplined repo with years of consistent process, or a scrappy weekend project three months old. Nothing about the output tells you which situation you're in.

## The gap

Hotspots' ranking leans partly on authorship patterns — files touched by a small, consistent set of people tend to predict future fixes differently than files with scattered, rotating ownership. That signal is real, but how well it works turns out to depend on the repo. A team that writes disciplined, Conventional-Commits-style messages tends to have other disciplined habits too, and in my evaluation, repos like that had a measurably more reliable version of this signal than repos without that habit. A team with messy, inconsistent commit history isn't doing anything wrong, necessarily — but the signal built on top of that history is, on average, a little less trustworthy there.

Up until v3.8.0, hotspots had no way to say any of that. Every repo got the same score with the same implied confidence.

## What v3.8.0 actually adds

Two new fields in the JSON `summary` output: `signal_confidence` (`"low"` or `"high"`), and `conventional_prefix_rate`, the raw number the label came from. I shipped them together on purpose. A label with no underlying number is something you have to take on faith; a label with the number right next to it is something you can check yourself — run `git log HEAD -n 500 --format=%s` on your own repo, count the lines that look like `feat:` or `fix(scope)!:`, and you've reproduced the exact rate hotspots used.

Neither field touches `file_risk_score`, any band, or any ranking order. That was a deliberate constraint, not an oversight — it's also the thing that makes this field different from several ideas I tried and abandoned before landing on it. I spent real effort trying to use commit-message hygiene (and a few related repo characteristics) to directly adjust the ranking — blend it into the score, gate a signal's weight by it. None of it moved a ranking by more than noise, on any sample I could construct fairly. What finally worked was giving up on changing the ranking at all, and just telling the user something true about how reliable it's likely to be. That's a much easier bar, and it's the one `signal_confidence` actually clears.

It's also deliberately non-sticky: recomputed fresh from the last 500 commits every single time you run `analyze`, never cached. Commit conventions are something a team picks up partway through a project, not a fixed trait — a stale cached value would be most wrong exactly on the repos where this matters most.

## How to read it

`"high"` means: lean on this repo's ranking a bit more than you might otherwise. `"low"` means: treat it as a rougher hint, and maybe look a little harder at files the ranking doesn't flag. Either way, it's a population-level calibration, not a certified accuracy rating for your specific repo — I found at least one repo in my own evaluation sample that landed in the "low" tier by commit convention while still having one of the most reliable authorship signals I measured anywhere. The label is a nudge in the right direction on average, not a guarantee for any one case.
