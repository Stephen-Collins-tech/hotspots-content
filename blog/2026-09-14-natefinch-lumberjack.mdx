Four of the five top functions are flagged 'exit_heavy' — meaning they have many distinct return points scattered through error-handling branches. In Go, every early return on an `err != nil` check is an explicit, visible fork, which is good for readability line-by-line but adds up to a real test-coverage burden once a function has a dozen of them. One function, `millRunOnce`, is also flagged 'god_function' and 'stale_complex' — the combination that matters most here, because it means the function both does too much and hasn't been revisited in years.

### `millRunOnce` — lumberjack.go

&lt;FunctionCard fn='millRunOnce' file='lumberjack.go' risk='12.33' band='critical' cc='42' nd='3' fo='15' touches='0' /&gt;

This is the highest-risk function in the repository, and it earns that spot on structure alone: a cyclomatic complexity of 42, a fan-out of 15 distinct calls, and no changes in 2077 days. Reading the source, it's doing three separate jobs in one body — trimming backups past 'MaxBackups', pruning files older than 'MaxAge', and deciding what needs compression — each with its own loop building up 'remove' and 'compress' slices, followed by two more loops that actually perform the removal and compression while accumulating the first non-nil error. That's the 'god_function' pattern in concrete terms: one function owns filtering, cutoff-time math, and I/O side effects, and fifteen distinct downstream calls means a change to any one concern risks touching the others. The file's commit history shows a bug-fix fraction of 0.2692 across 26 total commits and a review-comment density of 0.75, which tells me this file has drawn real reviewer attention in the past even though no individual commit is tagged as bug-linked. My recommendation: split this into three functions — one that computes what to remove, one that computes what to compress, and one that executes the file operations — before the next feature request touches retention policy.

### `compressLogFile` — lumberjack.go

&lt;FunctionCard fn='compressLogFile' file='lumberjack.go' risk='11.79' band='critical' cc='21' nd='1' fo='12' touches='0' /&gt;

Second on the list, with a cyclomatic complexity of 21 and a fan-out of 12, dormant for 838 days. The 'exit_heavy' tag is earned honestly here — I count at least eight separate error-returning checks across opening the source file, statting it, chowning the destination, opening the gzip destination, copying bytes, closing the writer, closing the reader, and removing the source. Each of those wraps its own `fmt.Errorf` with a distinct message, which helps diagnostics but means testing every failure path requires mocking eight different os/io failure points. The nesting depth of 1 is actually reassuring — this isn't deeply nested logic, it's a long, flat sequence of sequential operations, which makes it a good extract-method candidate: pull the open-stat-chown preamble into its own helper and leave the copy-and-cleanup as a second function.

### `openNew` — lumberjack.go

&lt;FunctionCard fn='openNew' file='lumberjack.go' risk='11.06' band='critical' cc='13' nd='2' fo='11' touches='0' /&gt;

A cyclomatic complexity of 13 and fan-out of 11, dormant for 838 days just like `compressLogFile` — both were last touched in the same window, suggesting they moved together in a prior refactor. The logic here handles directory creation, mode preservation from the old file, renaming the existing log to its backup name, a platform-specific chown, and finally opening the new file — five distinct concerns stacked in sequence with an early return baked into the middle for the 'file already exists' branch. It's not the worst offender in this file, but an 11-way fan-out for what is conceptually 'make way for a new log file' is worth trimming — I'd look at whether the chown and rename steps can be isolated into a single 'rotateExistingFile' helper called before the actual open.

### `Write` — lumberjack.go

&lt;FunctionCard fn='Write' file='lumberjack.go' risk='10.9' band='critical' cc='13' nd='2' fo='9' touches='0' /&gt;

`Write` is the public entry point of the `io.Writer` interface this whole package exists to implement, which makes its complexity worth extra scrutiny — a cyclomatic complexity of 13 and fan-out of 9, untouched for 3157 days, the longest dormancy of any function in the top five. The function guards a mutex, checks the write length against the configured maximum, lazily opens or rotates the file, and only then performs the actual write — four conditional gates before a single byte is written. Because this method is called on every log line an application emits, any latent edge case here has a wide blast radius, even though nothing in the data suggests it has misbehaved. I'd leave the mutex handling alone and focus any refactor on separating the size-check-and-rotate decision from the write itself, since that's the part most likely to need a new branch the next time file-size policy changes.

### `openExistingOrNew` — lumberjack.go

&lt;FunctionCard fn='openExistingOrNew' file='lumberjack.go' risk='10.04' band='critical' cc='11' nd='1' fo='11' touches='0' /&gt;

Rounding out the top five: cyclomatic complexity of 11 against a fan-out of 11 — a rare case where the number of distinct calls out roughly matches the number of decision branches, meaning almost every conditional path here ends in a call to another piece of rotation machinery ('mill', 'openNew', 'rotate'). It's been dormant for 838 days, the same window as `openNew` and `compressLogFile`, reinforcing that this trio of file-lifecycle functions was last worked on together. Given its role deciding between reuse, rotation, and fresh-file creation, I'd treat any future change to this function as a signal to re-review `openNew` and `rotate` in the same pass, since they're tightly coupled by call graph even if git hasn't linked them recently.

Stepping back, the pattern across all five is consistent: nothing here is broken, and nothing here is currently churning. What I'm looking at is a rotation-and-cleanup subsystem that was written once, works, and hasn't needed a human in years — exactly the profile that turns into an expensive incident when someone finally does have to change 'MaxAge' semantics or add a new backup-naming scheme. The bug-fix fraction of 0.2692 and review-comment density of 0.75 on this file suggest past changes here haven't been trivial to land cleanly, which is one more reason to shrink these functions before the next one arrives.
