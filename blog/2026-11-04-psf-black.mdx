### `normalize_invisible_parens` — linegen.py

<FunctionCard fn='normalize_invisible_parens' file='src/black/linegen.py' risk='3.18' band='critical' cc='84' nd='5' fo='29' touches='28' />

This is the top-ranked function, and I would start with it because a cyclomatic complexity of 84 puts it well into the extreme range while 28 touches in the last year and a last change 0 days ago show it is being edited constantly.

<MetricBar label='Cyclomatic Complexity' value='84' threshold='10' max='100' />

From the excerpt, the function does several different jobs in one body. It first scans the node's prefix comments and bails out early on a formatting-off directive. It then runs a block gated by a preview feature flag that branches on statement type (for loops, comprehensions, assignments, delete statements, with statements) and hands each case to an unpacking-normalization helper. After that it wraps with-statement context managers, and finally it loops over children with a series of type checks, recursing into itself for annotated assignments, case blocks and guards. The recursion matters: a change to one branch can affect how another branch is reached.

The fan-out of 29 is high, and in Python the real coupling is likely larger than the number suggests because the node types and helper behavior are resolved at runtime. The preview-flag gating adds a second axis of paths: each flag effectively doubles the states a test needs to cover. The file-level bug-fix rate of 54% and a review comment density of 3.0 are consistent with a function people argue about, but again they are file-level signals.

My recommendation: extract the preview-gated unpacking block into its own function keyed on statement type, and separate the early directive check. Those two moves are mechanical, preserve behavior, and should remove a large share of the 84 paths without touching the main child loop.

### `visit_power` — linegen.py

<FunctionCard fn='visit_power' file='src/black/linegen.py' risk='3.13' band='critical' cc='32' nd='8' fo='21' touches='28' />

The number I would look at here is the nesting depth of 8, which is the strong refactoring threshold and the deepest of the five. A cyclomatic complexity of 32 is lower than its neighbors, but depth makes it harder to reason about than the numbers alone suggest.

<MetricBar label='Max Nesting Depth' value='8' threshold='4' max='10' />

From the excerpt, it is a generator method that does two unrelated things: it wraps numeric literals in parentheses before an attribute access (skipping hex, binary, octal and complex literals), and it removes redundant parentheses around generators and subscripts when two preview flags are enabled. The second block is where the depth comes from: a loop over children, a trailer check, an if/elif split between generator and subscript handling, then more conditionals inside each branch for the inner rewrite.

Because it is a visitor method, the surrounding dispatch is implicit and resolved by node type at runtime, so I cannot tell from the data how many paths enter it. What the metrics do show is that the two jobs share no logic.

My recommendation: split it into a numeric-literal wrapping step and two helpers, one for generator trailers and one for subscript trailers, each using early `continue` to flatten the nesting. Getting depth from 8 to 3 or 4 is achievable with guard clauses alone.

### `delimiter_split` — linegen.py

<FunctionCard fn='delimiter_split' file='src/black/linegen.py' risk='3.12' band='critical' cc='36' nd='3' fo='23' touches='28' />

This is one of Black's line-splitting strategies, splitting on the highest-priority delimiter. A cyclomatic complexity of 36 and fan-out of 23 are both substantial, and the excerpt shows why: a sequence of early-exit checks that raise a 'cannot split' exception, several gated by preview features (keeping commented expressions together, hugging comparators, keeping dictionary keys with operators), followed by a nested local helper that appends leaves to the current line and then the main splitting loop.

Two things stand out. First, the number of distinct reasons to abandon a split is a test-coverage burden, since each raise is an exit path that needs its own input. Second, the nested helper closes over mutable state in the enclosing function, so reading the loop means tracking that state across the closure boundary.

My recommendation: move the preflight checks into a single function that either returns or raises, so the splitting body starts after a clean precondition boundary. That leaves the main loop and the helper to be reviewed on their own.

### `maybe_make_parens_invisible_in_atom` — linegen.py

<FunctionCard fn='maybe_make_parens_invisible_in_atom' file='src/black/linegen.py' risk='3.1' band='critical' cc='47' nd='3' fo='22' touches='28' />

A cyclomatic complexity of 47 with a nesting depth of only 3 tells me this is wide, not deep. The excerpt shows a single enormous guard condition that chains many `or` clauses, each testing a different node shape (empty tuples, one-tuples, yields, walrus-containing tuples, star expressions, generators, comparison operands, implicit string concatenation), with feature-flag and Python-version-feature checks folded in. Every `or` and `and` adds a path, which is how a function with shallow nesting reaches 47.

The function returns a boolean and takes eight parameters, several of them behavior-toggling booleans added over time (for example the generator-parens and subscript flags). That parameter list is itself a signal: each flag is a mode a caller can request, and `visit_power` in the same file is one of the callers passing them. The rest of the function appears to handle a walrus-assignment case with a list of parent types.

My recommendation: break the giant guard into named predicate functions such as 'is this atom's tuple unremovable' and 'is this a protected yield', then compose them. Naming the clauses makes each independently testable and makes the next preview flag a one-line addition instead of another branch in a 47-path function.

### `generate_trailers_to_omit` — linegen.py

<FunctionCard fn='generate_trailers_to_omit' file='src/black/linegen.py' risk='3.07' band='critical' cc='29' nd='4' fo='9' touches='28' />

This is the lowest-ranked of the five and the least coupled: a fan-out of 9 is modest, and the complexity is concentrated in its own control flow, with a cyclomatic complexity of 29 and a nesting depth of 4. It is a generator that walks a line's leaves in reverse, accumulating a cumulative set of closing-bracket IDs and yielding a new set each time a bracket pair can be omitted for a right-hand split.

The risk here is different from its neighbors. Low fan-out means few external dependencies to mock, but a generator with several `break` and `continue` exits, mutable accumulators (the omit set, the inner-bracket set, the tracked opening and closing brackets) and a trailing-comma rule duplicated in two places is hard to follow step by step. The duplicated trailing-comma check in the excerpt is a concrete consolidation target.

My recommendation: extract the repeated trailing-comma check into a small predicate and write table-driven tests that assert the exact sequence of yielded sets for a handful of bracket shapes. With this much implicit state, the yield order is the behavior worth pinning down before anyone refactors the loop.

**What the concentration means**

Taken together, the five functions share an unusual pattern: nearly all of them combine a large chain of boolean conditions with preview-feature gating. Each new preview feature adds branches to several of them at once, which would explain why a single file shows 28 touches and 14 recent authors. If I were planning refactoring work here, I would not schedule five separate tickets. I would pull the shared predicates, the preview-flag checks and the early-exit preconditions out of `linegen.py` first, then revisit the functions individually.
