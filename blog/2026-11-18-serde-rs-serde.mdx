Four of the five highest-ranked functions in serde-rs/serde are in the 'active' tier, so anyone shipping a change to serde_derive this week is working next to structurally complex code that is still moving. The top entry, `with_bound` in serde_derive/src/bound.rs, has an activity-weighted risk score of 0.8, a cyclomatic complexity of 12, nesting depth 4 and 4 touches in the last year. Two critical-band functions in serde_derive/src/internals/attr.rs, `from_ast` and `collect_lifetimes`, sit just behind it, each with a score near 0.7. I analyzed commit e306b0c, which has 1,196 functions in total and 12 in the critical band. I would start with `with_bound` and `from_ast`: `with_bound` has the top score in the set, and `from_ast` combines critical-band complexity with the widest coupling.

The table below ranks functions by **activity-weighted risk** — a score that multiplies structural complexity by recent commit frequency. A function that is both hard to understand (high cyclomatic complexity) and actively changing is a higher priority than one that is complex but untouched. CC = cyclomatic complexity (independent execution paths); ND = max nesting depth; FO = fan-out (distinct callees).

### `with_bound` — bound.rs

<FunctionCard fn='with_bound' file='serde_derive/src/bound.rs' risk='0.8' band='high' cc='12' nd='4' fo='6' touches='4' />

I would start with `with_bound` because it has the highest activity-weighted risk score in the repo (0.8) and it is in an active file with 4 touches in the last year. Its last change was 120 days ago. The file has 5 commits in total.

From the source excerpt, this function takes a container, a set of generics, a field filter and a bound path, and returns new generics. Inside it, a local `FindTyParams` visitor walks the syntax tree. It collects all generic type parameters, the ones used in fields that pass the filter, and fields whose types are associated types of a generic parameter. It includes a hardcoded exception for `PhantomData`, and a large `match` over `syn::Type` variants with an attribute that denies non-exhaustive patterns in exhaustive test builds.

The metrics (cyclomatic complexity 12, nesting depth 4, fan-out 6) are moderate on their own. The score is driven by how recent and frequent the changes are. What concerns me more than the numbers is the design: a visitor struct, its impl and the surrounding function are all nested inside a single function body. The `PhantomData` special case is exactly the sort of rule that gets extended when a new edge case turns up. My recommendation is to lift `FindTyParams` and its impl out to module level so the visitor can be unit-tested directly with small type fixtures, independent of the bound-building logic that calls it.

### `with_where_predicates_from_variants` — bound.rs

<FunctionCard fn='with_where_predicates_from_variants' file='serde_derive/src/bound.rs' risk='0.69' band='high' cc='7' nd='2' fo='3' touches='4' />

This is the simplest function in the top five, with cyclomatic complexity 7, nesting depth 2 and fan-out 3. Its activity-weighted risk score of 0.69 comes from sharing a file with `with_bound`: it also has 4 touches in the last year and 120 days since last change.

The excerpt shows an early return of the cloned generics for structs, then a loop over enum variants that copies where-predicates into the new where clause, skipping variants that provide none. I would not refactor this function. It is a worked example of how the rest of this file could look. I would use it as a review-ordering signal: when a change touches `with_bound`, check whether `with_where_predicates_from_variants` needs the same change, since both build `syn::Generics` that the derive output depends on.

### `from_ast` — attr.rs

<FunctionCard fn='from_ast' file='serde_derive/src/internals/attr.rs' risk='0.69' band='critical' cc='20' nd='5' fo='17' touches='2' />

`from_ast` is the one I would review most carefully for coupling. It is in the critical band with cyclomatic complexity 20, nesting depth 5 and fan-out 17, and it has an activity-weighted risk score of 0.69, 2 touches in the last year and 120 days since last change.

From the excerpt, this function extracts `#[serde(...)]` attributes from a struct field. It declares a long list of attribute accumulators up front (rename, aliases, skip flags, default, serialize_with, deserialize_with, bounds, borrow, getter, flatten). It then handles borrowed lifetimes, then loops over the field's attributes and parses nested metadata in a long `if`/`else if` chain keyed on the attribute path. A fan-out of 17 means a change to any of those helpers can ripple into this function, and a nesting depth of 5 means the branches inside the closure are hard to hold in your head.

The structure points to a concrete first step: extract the borrowed-lifetime block into its own function, and move each attribute-key branch in the parse closure into a small handler that takes the context and the meta item. That keeps the dispatch chain flat and gives each attribute a place to be tested on its own.

### `collect_lifetimes` — attr.rs

<FunctionCard fn='collect_lifetimes' file='serde_derive/src/internals/attr.rs' risk='0.67' band='critical' cc='34' nd='5' fo='4' touches='2' />

<MetricBar label='Cyclomatic Complexity' value='34' threshold='10' max='40' />

`collect_lifetimes` has the highest cyclomatic complexity in the top five at 34, with nesting depth 5 and fan-out 4. Its activity-weighted risk score is 0.67, with 2 touches in the last year and 120 days since last change.

The excerpt shows a recursive walk over `syn::Type`. Each type variant has its own arm, and the `Path` arm descends further into angle-bracketed generic arguments (lifetimes, types, associated types). Several arms end in an explicit no-op, followed by a catch-all. The cyclomatic complexity of 34 comes from the number of arms, not from tangled logic, and the low fan-out of 4 supports that reading. A recursive match like this is often a reasonable shape for the problem.

I would not split it for the sake of the number. The more useful move is a table-driven test that feeds one type of each variant and asserts the collected lifetimes. That test pins down the contract and makes the no-op arms visible, so whoever next adds a `syn` variant sees them.

### `check_internal_tag_field_name_conflict` — check.rs

<FunctionCard fn='check_internal_tag_field_name_conflict' file='serde_derive/src/internals/check.rs' risk='0.67' band='critical' cc='22' nd='5' fo='7' touches='1' />

This is the only quiet-tier function in the top five. It has cyclomatic complexity 22, nesting depth 5 and fan-out 7, an activity-weighted risk score of 0.67, 1 touch in the last year and 360 days since last change. It was last changed 360 days ago, so this reads as structural debt rather than live regression risk. It would carry a high blast radius the next time someone edits it.

From the excerpt, it validates that a field name in an internally tagged enum variant does not collide with the tag. It returns early for structs and for non-internal tag types, builds a small closure to emit the error, then loops over variants and fields, checking serialized names and deserialization aliases while honoring skip attributes. A nesting depth of 5 for what is a conflict check is higher than I would expect. The early `return` after each diagnosis also means the function reports at most one conflict per enum.

A concrete first step is to extract a helper that answers 'does this field conflict with the tag?' and returns a bool. The loops then collapse to one call per field, and the helper can be tested with a table of serialize/deserialize/skip combinations. That would cut nesting by about half without changing behavior.

If someone refactors validation in this file, I would look at the neighboring checks at the same time.

## Where to start

I would take them in this order. First, `with_bound`: lift the visitor out of the function body and add direct tests for it, since it has the highest score and sits in a file with 4 touches in the last year. Second, `from_ast`: its fan-out of 17 and nesting depth of 5, in active code, make it the function where a small edit is most likely to have an unexpected effect. Third, a table-driven test for `collect_lifetimes`, which costs little. Last, `check_internal_tag_field_name_conflict` in check.rs, which can wait until someone has a reason to be in that file, but should be cleaned up before that work starts.

Outside the top five, there are further critical-band functions in attr.rs and de/struct_.rs that I would look at next.
