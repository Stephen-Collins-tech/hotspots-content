### `hooksForLevel` — logger.go

<FunctionCard fn='hooksForLevel' file='logger.go' risk='1.83' band='active' cc='3' nd='1' fo='5' touches='6' />

I would start with `hooksForLevel` because it has the highest activity-weighted risk score of the five at 1.83, a fan-out of 5 (tied with `PanicFn`), and it was touched 6 times in the last year, most recently 0 days ago. From the excerpt, it takes the logger's mutex, reads the hook slice for a level, and returns a copy rather than the shared slice. It has an early-return branch for the empty case, which accounts for its CC of 3 and nesting depth of 1.

The complexity is not the concern here. The concern is that this function sits on a concurrency boundary: it guards shared state with a lock and hands back a copy. Go's goroutine interactions are exactly the kind of risk structural metrics cannot see. The file-level history is quiet: no bug-linked commits, no reverts, a bug-fix rate of 0%, 6 total commits, and 1 distinct author in the last 90 days. So I frame this as review-attention debt, not a defect signal. My recommendation: make sure there is a test that exercises hook registration and level lookup concurrently under the race detector, so edits to the locking pattern are caught.

### `newEntry` — logger.go

<FunctionCard fn='newEntry' file='logger.go' risk='1.8' band='active' cc='3' nd='1' fo='2' touches='6' />

`newEntry` has an activity-weighted risk score of 1.8, a fan-out of only 2, and the same 6 touches in the last year. It appears to pull an entry from a pool with a type assertion and fall back to constructing a new one when the assertion fails. The companion `releaseEntry` clears the entry's data and returns it to the pool, and many methods in this file pair the two calls.

The pairing is the part worth reviewing. Every public logging method in logger.go depends on acquiring and releasing an entry correctly, and some do it with a deferred release while others release explicitly after the call. I would want that pattern consistent, because a change to pool behavior here ripples across dozens of call sites. As with the other four, the history shows no defect signal: 0 bug-linked commits and 0 reverts. My recommendation: add a small helper or test that asserts acquire and release stay balanced across the method families, so a future edit to one variant does not silently diverge from the others.

**`PanicFn`, `Panic`, and `Panicf` — logger.go**

I am grouping these three because they share one shape. Each checks whether the panic level is enabled, acquires an entry, defers its release, and delegates to the matching entry method. `PanicFn` has a fan-out of 5 and scores 1.8; `Panic` and `Panicf` each have a fan-out of 4 and score 1.79. All three have a CC of 3 and nesting depth of 1, and all three were touched 6 times in the last year with 0 days since last change.

The notable detail is the deferred release. The panic variants use a deferred release, which makes sense for a path that panics, while the non-panic variants such as `Log` and `Print` release explicitly after the call. The excerpts document in comments that Log, Logf, and LogFn at the panic and fatal levels intentionally do not panic or exit. It is also exactly the sort of subtlety a future edit can break. A panic path that skips its deferred release leaks a pooled entry, and I would not trust a reviewer to catch that by eye in a 3-way copy of near-identical code.

My recommendation: write one table-driven test covering Panic, Panicf, Panicln, and PanicFn together, asserting both that the panic fires and that the entry is returned to the pool. Three near-identical functions with near-identical scores are a sign that they should be tested as a family, not individually.

## Where to start

I would open with `hooksForLevel`, because its locking and copy-on-read behavior is the one place in this file where a subtle edit could produce a concurrency bug that the metrics cannot show. Then I would add the family test for the four panic-path functions and the acquire/release balance check for `newEntry`.

Two points of context keep this in proportion. First, none of these five is structurally heavy. The heavier structural debt in logrus is elsewhere and quiet: `parseLevel` in logrus.go (CC 16, 145 days since last change), `String` in logrus.go (CC 16, 145 days), `appendAttr` in hooks/slog/handler.go (CC 15, 97 days), and `Handle` in the same file (CC 12, fan-out 11, 97 days). Those carry a high blast radius when next touched, but they are not changing now. Second, logger.go is where the changes are happening today, so it is where review attention pays off first. If the slog hook files start moving, their priority will rise quickly.
