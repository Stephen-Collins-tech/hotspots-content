Because the same file-level history applies to all five, I treat the file as the unit of review here, not any single function.

A function that is both hard to understand (high cyclomatic complexity) and actively changing is a higher priority than one that is complex but untouched. CC = cyclomatic complexity (independent execution paths); ND = max nesting depth; FO = fan-out (distinct callees).

Two patterns run through the top five. Two functions (`highlight_line_respects_no_color_on_terminal` and `sanitize_auto_sanitizes_plain_text_on_terminal`) drive bat through a pseudo-terminal and repeat nearly identical plumbing. Three (`config_terminator_escape_options_keep_working`, `config_terminator_filenames_preserve_config_and_bat_opts`, and `config_terminator_help_filenames_preserve_config_errors`) form a cluster around config-terminator behavior, built from nested loops over option and filename lists. I will take them in that order.

### `highlight_line_respects_no_color_on_terminal` — integration_tests.rs

<FunctionCard fn='highlight_line_respects_no_color_on_terminal' file='tests/integration_tests.rs' risk='3.42' band='critical' cc='9' nd='5' fo='9' touches='62' />

This is the top-ranked function, with an activity-weighted risk score of 3.42. I would start with it because it combines a nesting depth of 5 with a fan-out of 9 in a function that has been touched 62 times in the last year and was changed 0 days ago.

From the excerpt, it iterates over wrap modes, themes, and a NO_COLOR setting, and for each combination it opens a PTY, spawns bat with a long argument list, waits with a timeout, drains the PTY master through a poll loop, and then branches on whether NO_COLOR was set. That is three nested `for` loops with a nested read loop inside, which explains the depth of 5. It also pulls in `nix` APIs and raw file descriptors under `#[cfg(unix)]`, so a reader has to track descriptor lifetimes (the comment about keeping a slave open on macOS suggests this is a known hazard) alongside the test's actual assertion.

My recommendation: extract the PTY spawn-and-drain sequence into a helper that takes the argument list and environment and returns the captured stdout bytes. The test body then collapses to the loops plus two assertions, and the nesting drops by at least two levels.

### `sanitize_auto_sanitizes_plain_text_on_terminal` — integration_tests.rs

<FunctionCard fn='sanitize_auto_sanitizes_plain_text_on_terminal' file='tests/integration_tests.rs' risk='3.26' band='critical' cc='5' nd='2' fo='8' touches='62' />

This one scores 3.26, but its shape is different: a cyclomatic complexity of only 5 and a nesting depth of 2, with a fan-out of 8. The risk here is not branching. It is that the function repeats the same PTY setup, spawn, timeout, and drain sequence that appears in the top-ranked function, line for line as far as the excerpt shows.

That duplication is the real finding. Two functions in a file with a bug-linked history each carry their own copy of a subtle, platform-sensitive procedure. A fix to the drain loop or the timeout handling has to be applied twice, and it is easy to apply it once. The same extracted helper I recommended above would serve both. This is the cheapest refactor in the list and it reduces risk in two of the five hotspots at once.

**`config_terminator_filenames_preserve_config_and_bat_opts` and `config_terminator_help_filenames_preserve_config_errors` — integration_tests.rs**

<FunctionCard fn='config_terminator_help_filenames_preserve_config_errors' file='tests/integration_tests.rs' risk='3.22' band='critical' cc='6' nd='4' fo='4' touches='62' />

I group these two because they share a shape: a cyclomatic complexity of 6, a nesting depth of 4, and three or four levels of `for` loops that write fixture files and then run a command. The first (risk 3.27) varies the BAT_OPTS environment and a list of filenames that look like flags, then expects `second\nsecond\n` on stdout. The second (risk 3.22) varies the config content, whether BAT_OPTS is used, and the filenames `--help` and `-h`, expecting specific exit codes and error text.

Neither is complex in the cyclomatic sense. The cost is the nesting: to understand what a single assertion is checking, a reader has to hold three loop variables and an `if` that conditionally sets an environment variable. These are the lowest-risk items in the top five, and I would leave them until the PTY helper and the escape-options table are done. For a quick win here, I would flatten the loops with a small cartesian-product helper, which would cut the nesting depth in both.

## Where to start

I would work in this order. First, extract the shared PTY spawn-and-drain helper, which removes duplicated platform-sensitive code from `highlight_line_respects_no_color_on_terminal` and `sanitize_auto_sanitizes_plain_text_on_terminal` at once. Second, convert `config_terminator_escape_options_keep_working` to a table-driven test, since it has the highest cyclomatic complexity of the five at 16. Third, flatten the loops in the two remaining config-terminator tests.

The case for doing this now rather than later is the activity data: the file has 62 touches in the last year, 22 distinct authors in the last 90 days, and a change as recent as today. Each concurrent contributor adding a case to these loops raises the odds of a merge conflict or a mis-copied branch. I am not saying the tests are wrong. I am saying that this file will keep absorbing changes, and its current shape makes each one harder to review than it needs to be.
