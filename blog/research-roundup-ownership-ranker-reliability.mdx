[Last time](/blog/file-risk-top-decile-findings), I wrote about `file_risk_score` failing to
calibrate as a probability but still holding up as a relative, diff-scoped signal: a file that
lands in the top decile of `file_risk_score` among the other files in the same diff is 6-7x
more likely to need a later fix than one that doesn't — not a guess, a result that held up on
five repos the original work never touched. That's still the standout result in this project
so far, and it's the one now shipping on every `hotspots diff` as `file_risk_top_decile`. This
post is a round-up of four smaller results, not a successor to it.

This one covers four more research results that already shipped quietly over the past several
months — none of them big enough on their own for a full post, but all of them worth stating
plainly, because together they say something about how this tool gets built: every formula in
it is checked against real outcomes on real repos, on a schedule, including after it ships.
Sometimes that check says "cut it."

All four are written up in full, with the actual numbers, in
[hotspots-public-research](https://github.com/Stephen-Collins-tech/hotspots-public-research) —
alongside the [top-decile finding](https://github.com/Stephen-Collins-tech/hotspots-public-research/blob/main/docs/findings/F257-F262-top-decile-flag.md)
itself. This post is the short version of the newer four.

---

## Two terms got cut from the live score

The activity-risk formula that `hotspots analyze` reports isn't static. It gets re-measured,
and terms that don't earn their weight get removed — even after they ship. Two more came out
this round, for two different reasons.

The first, `depth_score`, measures how deep a function sits in the call graph. It sounded
reasonable. It wasn't pulling its weight: across six tested repos, it was populated for
somewhere between half a percent and twenty percent of functions, and its average
contribution to the formula's overall ranking quality was *negative* — a net drag, not a
contributor. The root cause traces straight to the call-graph walk itself: it starts from a
small set of name-matched "entry points" (functions like `main`, `run`, request handlers),
and most functions in most repos simply aren't reachable from that narrow root set. This is
the third term removed for exactly this reason — two others (fan-in, strongly-connected
components) were already zeroed out earlier for the same structural-sparsity problem. At this
point it looks like a pattern, not a fluke.

The second, `convention_bug_fix_count`, is more interesting, because the problem wasn't
weakness — it was a quiet correctness bug. This feature counts how many times a function has
been touched by a commit that looks like a bug fix, and it's meant to flag ongoing risk. When
I actually swept it through the trained ranker's feature space, it turned out to **ratchet**:
in both model classes the ranker uses, increasing this feature's value essentially never
decreased the predicted score. A function touched by one fix commit, years ago, stays flagged
as elevated risk forever, no matter what else about it changes. That's not a risk signal, it's
a scar that never fades. Removed from the feature set entirely.

## Ownership concentration is one of the cleanest signals in the whole project

`hotspots coordinate` already reported how concentrated a file's authorship is. The open
question was whether that concentration — one dominant author vs. several, vs. no clear owner
at all — could be classified reliably, and whether it predicted anything useful on its own.

The "one dominant author" case turned out to be a clean, zero-tuning-needed classifier: it
matched an independent cross-check with 100 percent accuracy, on every repo tested, across
every threshold swept. The "no clear owner" case did not — accuracy ranged all the way down to
under 6 percent on one repo. Tracing that down to a cause (not just noticing the number was
bad) turned up a real bug in how author identity gets counted: a maintainer who committed under
two different email addresses over the years looks, to an email-keyed metric, like five or six
different people wrote the file. A genuinely single-owner file gets misclassified as
ownerless. That's a real limitation in the underlying signal, not an artifact of this one test
— so the "no clear owner" case shipped as deliberately unimplemented, not shipped-and-hoped.

The concentration classifier that *did* work turned out to be a strong, direct predictor of
something practical: how often a file sees cross-author collisions in its own history. Files
with one dominant author collide at roughly one to eleven percent the rate of otherwise
similar files with no dominant author — a thirty-to-sixty-percentage-point gap, consistent
across eight different public repos. `hotspots coordinate` now uses this to soften its
serialize-this-work recommendation when every file in a change set is concentrated.

## Making the trained ranker fail safely instead of failing silently

The most uncomfortable thing I found in this stretch of work: a tabular ranker can look
basically fine by one measure (overall rank correlation) while being completely useless by
the measure that actually matters — precision in the top handful of results, which is what a
person actually looks at. On one real repo, every ranker tested put zero real future bugs in
its top ten predictions, worse than if you'd picked at random. The reason was almost poetic:
the highest-churn, highest-complexity, most-recently-touched code on that repo was its most
heavily-exercised, most carefully-maintained core — and the actual bugs lived in quieter,
peripheral code that none of the usual signals were pointed at.

Rank correlation would never have caught this. A gate that specifically checks precision at
the *top* of a ranker's own output does catch it, reliably, with no false positives on the
repos where the ranker already works — and when it fires, it falls back to a cheap classifier
that recovers the lost precision. There's also a cheaper, upstream version of the same idea:
a quick pre-training check that predicts, before you spend the time training a repo-specific
model at all, whether it's going to be worth it. That screener matched the real outcome on
every repo in its known-answer set.

And in the same vein — sometimes the right move is to never add a feature in the first place.
A coupling metric (how many other files tend to change alongside this one) looked useful in
isolation, but its sign flips depending on how a given repo tends to batch its commits: in
repos with large, sweeping commits, high coupling just means "touched by big commits," not
"genuinely risky." Thirteen repos, one statistically significant but repo-dependent sign-flip,
no clean universal threshold. It never made it into the trained ranker's default feature set,
and checking why confirmed that was the right call.

## A fallback for repos with thin history

The last piece: what do you do with a repo that doesn't have enough history yet for any of
this to work well? Two things turned out to matter. First, a single cheap number — the Gini
coefficient of how concentrated a repo's commits are across its files — tracks pretty well
how hard that repo is for a history-light model to rank (a 0.73 correlation across eleven
public repos). A repo where commits spread evenly across everything is a repo where a
pooled model struggles to tell risky files from ordinary ones.

Second, and more useful in practice: on exactly those hard, evenly-spread repos, a
label-free anomaly detector running on cheap activity features — commit count, author count,
burst patterns, recency — still finds something real. On several low-Gini repos it hit
perfect or near-perfect precision in its top ten, up to a nineteen-times lift over the repo's
own base rate, using no labels at all. On repos where the normal pooled model already works
fine, the same technique adds nothing — which is exactly what you'd want from a fallback: it
helps where the primary approach is weak, and gets out of the way where it isn't.

---

Four results, four shipped changes, two of them removals. None of this is the kind of thing
that makes for a dramatic headline on its own. Together, though, it's the actual process: the
formula gets checked against real repos on a schedule, the checks sometimes say "this term is
dead weight" or "this feature has a hidden bug," and the tool changes because of it. That's
the whole point of keeping the research repo public — so the claim "we checked" isn't just
something to take on faith.
