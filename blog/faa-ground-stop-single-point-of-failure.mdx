On November 13, 2026, a single piece of newly installed network equipment at a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas set off a nationwide ground stop that cancelled somewhere between 12,000 and 20,000 flights, depending on whose count you use. Instability spread to 17 of the FAA's 22 en-route air traffic control centers. The FAA says legacy equipment destabilized under a flood of roughly 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. Four hours and twenty-one minutes later, it was over. Nobody has agreed yet on whose fault it was.

That disagreement is the most useful part of the story.

## One Router, Two Explanations

L3Harris, the contractor managing the FAA's telecom infrastructure, was in the middle of a routine and overdue upgrade: replacing copper lines that were being discontinued with fiber, and installing a new router - a Juniper device, made by HPE - at the El Dorado site to handle the switch. A second router installed about three weeks earlier at a radar site in Amarillo, Texas had already been in service without incident. When the El Dorado router came online, both routers began flooding the network with connection attempts, and the FAA's legacy backbone couldn't absorb it.

HPE's position is that the device didn't fail - it did exactly what it was configured to do. The air traffic controllers' union has pointed at the installation itself, suggesting the usual change-management protocol wasn't followed during the upgrade. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has said investigators are weighing contractor installation error against faulty equipment. As of this writing, nobody has published a root cause, and the two most likely explanations - "the hardware is defective" and "the hardware was configured wrong" - point to two completely different fixes.

## Why That Ambiguity Is the Real Finding

A hardware defect is a vendor problem: swap the part, qualify a different one, done. A configuration error traceable to a change-management gap is a process problem, and it's a much less comfortable one to own, because it means the failure mode was always available to any future install that skips the same step. The FAA has paused further Juniper router deployments while the investigation continues - which is the correct caution, but it's also a decision made without knowing which of the two explanations is true. If it's a config issue and not a hardware defect, pausing the rollout treats the symptom and leaves the actual gap - whatever let an install bypass protocol - wide open for the next contractor, the next router, the next site.

What makes this a single point of failure worth naming, rather than just a vendor's bad day, is the blast radius a change at one radio site had on a nationwide system. Legacy equipment at 17 of 22 en-route centers destabilized because of what happened at one location in Arkansas during a change nobody flagged as unusually risky going in - it was a routine copper-to-fiber swap, the kind of maintenance work that happens constantly and rarely makes news. The piece of infrastructure that turned out to be structurally load-bearing for the whole network wasn't obviously special until the day it mattered.

## The Same Question, Asked Earlier

This is the same gap [the Microsoft 365 outage three days earlier](/blog/microsoft-365-outage-lock-contention) exposed in a software gateway instead of a network router: a component with a disproportionate blast radius relative to how routine the change touching it looked, where nothing in the process flagged "this one needs more scrutiny than a normal change" before it shipped. Microsoft's defect sat dormant for three months because the test hardware didn't match production load. The FAA's router flooded the network immediately because - on either explanation - something about this specific install differed from the ones that came before it without being treated as higher-risk at the time.

[Hotspots](https://hotspots.dev) exists for the code version of this problem: ranking functions and files by activity-weighted risk so that changes to structurally central, high-fan-in code get scrutiny proportional to their blast radius, not proportional to how routine the change looks on the ticket. It won't configure a router correctly or catch a contractor skipping a change-management step. But the underlying failure in both incidents is the same: a component whose criticality wasn't visible until it broke, changed by a process that had no mechanism for treating "routine" and "load-bearing" as two different things that can both be true of the same change.
