Nobody has touched `ServeHTTP` in `static.go` for 1858 days, and it is still the riskiest function in negroni. That makes it structural debt rather than an emergency: with a cyclomatic complexity of 26, a fan-out of 13, and an activity-weighted risk score of 13.64, the next developer to open it will be reading five-year-old branching logic with no recent commit history to learn from. Negroni is a small Go middleware library, and across its 46 functions only 1 lands in the critical band, with all 4 top hotspots sitting in the debt quadrant and none in the fire quadrant.

The table below ranks functions by **activity-weighted risk** — a score that multiplies structural complexity by recent commit frequency. A function that is both hard to understand (high cyclomatic complexity) and actively changing is a higher priority than one that is complex but untouched. CC = cyclomatic complexity (independent execution paths); ND = max nesting depth; FO = fan-out (distinct callees).

### `ServeHTTP` — static.go

<FunctionCard fn='ServeHTTP' file='static.go' risk='13.64' band='critical' cc='26' nd='3' fo='13' touches='0' />

I would start here because this function has the highest activity-weighted risk score in the repository, 13.64, and it has gone 1858 days since last change, with zero touches in the last year. The handler is flagged as exit-heavy, a god function, and stale-complex, and the source excerpt shows why. One method decides whether a request is a GET or HEAD, applies and validates a URL prefix, opens the file from the configured directory, stats it, handles the directory case (a redirect for a missing trailing slash, including a double-slash cleanup, then an index file lookup), and finally calls the standard content-serving routine.

<MetricBar label='Cyclomatic Complexity' value='26' threshold='10' max='30' />

A cyclomatic complexity of 26 means roughly 26 paths to cover in tests. Most of those paths end in the same action, handing the request to the next handler, which is what drives the exit-heavy pattern. In Go this is the idiomatic early-return style, and nesting stays at 3, so the function is wide rather than deep. The cost is test burden: each early exit is a separate case, and several depend on filesystem errors that are awkward to trigger. The file has only 2 commits in its history, no bug-linked commits, no reverts, and no authors in the last 90 days, so I read this as maintainability debt, not a defect signal.

My recommendation: extract the prefix handling into a small helper that returns the cleaned path and a boolean, and move the directory and index-file resolution into a second helper. The main method would shrink to the method check, the two helper calls, and the final serve call, and the helpers can be table-tested without a real filesystem.

### `ServeHTTP` — recovery.go

<FunctionCard fn='ServeHTTP' file='recovery.go' risk='8.74' band='high' cc='2' nd='3' fo='15' touches='0' />

**Recovery middleware**

This is the panic-recovery middleware, and its risk is of a different kind. Its cyclomatic complexity is only 2, but its fan-out is 15 and it is flagged as a god function. The deferred closure does a lot: it builds the panic information, captures a stack trace, wraps the response writer, chooses between a formatted error response and a plain 500, optionally logs, and then invokes two optional user-supplied callbacks, each wrapped in its own nested recover. Much of the branching lives inside the deferred anonymous function and the nested callback wrappers, so the low complexity number understates the structure.

The file has more history than the others on this list: 18 commits in total, a bug-fix rate of 28%, 2 reverts, and 1 distinct author in the last 90 days. Those are file-level signals and do not prove this function is defective, but it is also the most recently changed of the four, at 91 days since last change, with no touches in the last year. I would be most careful about edits here, because the behavior it protects only runs when something has already gone wrong, which is exactly when you do not want surprises.

I would extract the response-writing branch and the two guarded callback invocations into named helpers. The two callback wrappers are nearly identical, so a single helper that runs a function under a recover and logs on failure would remove duplication and lower fan-out.

**`detectAddress` and `build` — negroni.go**

<FunctionCard fn='detectAddress' file='negroni.go' risk='6.44' band='high' cc='5' nd='1' fo='2' touches='0' />

<FunctionCard fn='build' file='negroni.go' risk='6.4' band='high' cc='6' nd='1' fo='4' touches='0' />

These two share a file, a history, and a profile, so I am taking them together. Neither is structurally severe: cyclomatic complexity of 5 and 6, nesting depth of 1, and fan-out of 2 and 4. They make the top list because they have sat untouched for a very long time, 3490 days for `detectAddress` and 2705 days for `build`, and they have gone untouched for years. The file-level history is modest: 14 commits total, a bug-fix rate of about 7%, no reverts, and no authors in the last 90 days.

`detectAddress` resolves a listen address from an explicit argument, then the PORT environment variable, then a default. `build` constructs the middleware chain recursively from the handler list, with a switch over empty, multiple, and single handler cases. Both read in under a minute, and I would not schedule refactoring for either. Instead I would confirm that tests exist for the empty-list and single-handler paths in `build`, and for the environment-variable fallback in `detectAddress`, since the chain construction underpins every request that passes through the library.

**What I would do with this list**

The priority order is `ServeHTTP` in `static.go` first, because it is the only critical-band function and the one with the most paths to test. Second is `ServeHTTP` in `recovery.go`, for its fan-out and its file-level history of reverts. The two `negroni.go` functions are low-effort test-coverage checks, not refactors.
