> **Updated June 10, 2026:** Added the cross-repo density correlation statistic (Pearson r=−0.679, p=0.011 across 13 codebases) that quantifies the sign-flip pattern. The original post described the inversion without reporting the underlying regression.

Files that change together are coupled. Coupled files share logic, share dependencies, and — intuitively — share risk. A file that co-changes with half the codebase should be riskier than one that changes in isolation.

This intuition is right on some codebases and completely backwards on others. Here's how I found that out, twice.

---

## Experiment 1: Count how many files it co-changes with

The simplest coupling signal: for each file, count the number of distinct files it has ever changed alongside in the same commit. Call it co-change count.

I measured this across 13 open-source codebases and correlated it with defect labels. On smaller, focused repos — a Go web framework, a Python microframework — the correlation was positive. Files with many co-change partners tended to be the ones that later needed bug fixes. The intuition held.

On larger, more active repos — Svelte, React — the correlation went negative. Files with the most co-change partners were the *least* likely to be defective. The signal had inverted. Across the full 13-repo set, the relationship between repo coupling density and signal direction was stark: Pearson r=−0.679 (p=0.011) between mean co-change partners per file and per-repo signal ρ. The denser the codebase, the more anti-predictive the signal became.

The explanation: in a large, active codebase, most commits touch many files at once. Config files, changelogs, build scripts, type definitions — they appear constantly, in nearly every commit. A file with thousands of co-change partners in that context is not a fragile, deeply-coupled component. It is a frequently-updated peripheral file that gets swept up in bulk commits.

Co-change count was not measuring coupling. On dense repos, it was measuring how often a file appears in large batch commits. High score meant busy, not risky.

---

## Experiment 2: Normalise for activity rates

My hypothesis: the problem is that raw counts don't adjust for each file's baseline activity. A file that appears in 90% of commits has high co-change counts by construction. Normalised mutual information (NMI) conditions on both files' marginal activity rates — if A appears in 90% of commits and B appears in 90% of commits, their NMI is low because the co-change isn't surprising.

I computed pairwise NMI across commit histories and took the maximum NMI per file as its coupling score.

The results were worse:

| Codebase | ρ NMI | ρ raw co-change | ρ activity baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Svelte | −0.349 | +0.023 | +0.692 |
| React | −0.160 | +0.237 | +0.253 |
| Django | −0.550 | +0.078 | +0.660 |
| Gin (Go) | +0.032 | −0.023 | +0.091 |
| scikit-learn | +0.117 | +0.514 | +0.346 |

NMI made the large-codebase problem structurally worse. On Svelte it went from +0.023 to −0.349. On Django from +0.078 to −0.550.

The reason: taking the maximum NMI over all partners almost always selects coupling to the most active files in the codebase. Those files have high mutual information with many partners precisely because their presence in a commit is predictable from everyone else. Max NMI finds the most deeply-embedded files and ranks them highly. Those files are not fragile — they are infrastructure.

Normalisation addressed the wrong problem. The issue wasn't that busy files had inflated counts. The issue was that coupling to busy files is not the same as coupling to fragile files.

---

## Experiment 3: Ask a different question

I stopped trying to fix the metric and changed the question.

Both previous experiments asked: *how coupled is this file to other files?* Dense repos broke both because coupling to infrastructure is not the same as coupling to defect risk.

The new question: *does this file tend to co-change with files that historically break?*

For each file, I computed a directed coupling score:

```
score(file) = sum over co-change partners of [ co-change count × partner defect rate ]
              divided by own commit count
```

A file that co-changes frequently with defect-prone partners scores high. A file that co-changes exclusively with stable config files scores near zero, regardless of how active it is.

| Codebase | ρ directed | ρ raw co-change | ρ activity baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Svelte | **+0.776** | +0.023 | +0.692 |
| React | **+0.260** | +0.237 | +0.253 |
| Django | **+0.452** | +0.078 | +0.660 |
| Gin (Go) | **+0.213** | −0.023 | +0.091 |
| scikit-learn | +0.462 | +0.514 | +0.346 |

The sign-flip is gone. The signal is positive on every codebase with sufficient labeled data. On Svelte — where the previous coupling signals were near-zero or negative — directed coupling (+0.776) now exceeds the activity baseline (+0.692).

---

## Then I checked for leakage

Strong full-history numbers invite suspicion. Partner defect rates and evaluation labels are both derived from commit history. If they come from the same window, the signal is partly circular.

I split each codebase's history at the 80th-percentile commit timestamp. Partner defect rates were computed from the earlier 80%. Labels came from the final 20% only.

| Codebase | ρ holdout | ρ full history | leakage? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Svelte | −0.033 | +0.776 | yes |
| React | +0.013 | +0.260 | yes |
| Gin (Go) | **+0.309** | +0.213 | low |
| Django | **+0.565** | +0.452 | low |
| scikit-learn | −0.222 | +0.462 | yes |

Three codebases collapse. The full-history numbers for Svelte, React, and scikit-learn were largely explained by circular label dependency.

Two codebases hold or improve. Django's holdout ρ (+0.565) exceeds its full-history number and is competitive with the activity baseline on the same holdout window (+0.594). Gin holdout (+0.309) also exceeds its full-history number — a sign the signal is conservative, not inflated.

---

## What separates the two groups

The split tracks label temporal distribution. Django and Gin have defect-fixing commits distributed across their full histories — training-window partner scores are a valid predictor of holdout-window risk.

Svelte, React, and scikit-learn appear to have defect activity concentrated in the recent period. When most labeled fixes happened in the last 20% of history, training-window partner scores don't predict holdout-window risk — because the defect pattern itself has shifted.

This is not unique to coupling signals. Any signal that depends on defect labels faces this problem on codebases where labeling is temporally uneven. It is a screener condition, not a death sentence for the approach.

---

## Where this leaves coupling as a signal

**Raw co-occurrence** is unreliable across codebase sizes. It is a reasonable heuristic on small, focused repos and a noise source on large, active ones.

**Normalisation-based fixes** don't address the root problem. The issue is not the similarity metric. Coupling to busy files and coupling to fragile files are different things.

**Directed coupling** is stable on codebases where defect labels are distributed across history. It computes in a single pass over co-occurrence data, scales with the number of co-occurring pairs rather than the quadratic number of all possible pairs, and degrades gracefully when partner scores are sparse.

Three experiments, wrong twice, conditionally right the third time. The condition — whether defect history is temporally distributed — turns out to matter more than the choice of metric.
