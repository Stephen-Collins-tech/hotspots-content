## Claim

Spearman ρ measures average rank agreement across all files. Precision@K measures
whether the files engineers actually review are in the right place. On mature, stable
codebases, a ranker can show positive ρ while returning zero bugs in its top 10.
P@K exposes failures that ρ hides.

---

## Reader Problem

You built a defect risk ranker. You evaluated it with Spearman ρ. The number is positive.
You ship it. Engineers review the top-10 highest-risk files. None of them have bugs.

This is not a hypothetical.

---

## The Setup

I run a risk ranker across multiple open-source codebases and report Spearman ρ as the
primary evaluation metric — rank correlation between predicted risk and observed defect
history. I added Precision@K as a secondary check: of the top K files the ranker surfaces,
what fraction actually had future defects?

On most repos these metrics agree. On one repo they disagreed badly enough that I had to
rethink what I was measuring.

---

## What Happened on scikit-learn

scikit-learn is a mature scientific Python library. Base defect rate in the holdout period:
21%. A random ranker surfaces defective files 21% of the time at any K.

Spearman ρ (exact-split holdout — same time window for training and labels):

| Ranker | Spearman ρ |
|---|---|
| Composite risk score | positive |
| XGBoost (repo-trained) | **+0.787** |

Precision@K (temporal holdout — future bugs only from post-2024 commits):

| Ranker | P@10 | P@20 | P@50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Random (base rate) | 0.210 | 0.210 | 0.210 |
| Composite risk score | **0.000** | 0.000 | 0.260 |
| Activity baseline | **0.000** | 0.050 | 0.100 |
| XGBoost (repo-trained) | **0.000** | 0.000 | **0.000** |

Note: the ρ and P@K numbers come from different holdout constructions and can't be
combined into one row. The conclusion holds across both: ρ registers XGBoost as
impressive (+0.787) while P@K shows its top-100 predictions contain zero future bugs.

All rankers return P@10 = 0. The composite score and activity baseline both show positive
Spearman ρ — they are correct in aggregate across all files — but their top 10 contain
zero future bugs.

XGBoost is worse. Trained on this repo's own history with the full feature set, it achieves
P@100 = 0. Its top hundred predictions contain no bugs at all. It learned the bad signal
more aggressively than the formula and is more confidently wrong.

The score distribution explains why. The composite risk score produces only three distinct
values across all functions in scikit-learn:

| Score bucket | Functions | Future bugs | Bug rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | 17 | 0 | **0%** |
| Middle | 67 | 14 | 21% |
| Bottom | 546 | 118 | 22% |

The top bucket has zero bugs. The middle and bottom are indistinguishable from random. The
ranker has confidently put the wrong files at the top.

---

## Why This Happens

The functions with the highest activity, complexity, and recency on scikit-learn are core
stable APIs — base classes in the linear model module, heavily maintained estimators,
central data structures. These are among the most carefully stewarded files in the codebase.
They accumulate changes, but almost never regress.

The real bugs live in less-trafficked code: edge cases in pipeline handling, rarely-touched
integration paths, feature extraction utilities that rarely get attention. These functions
have low churn, low complexity scores, and look safe by every signal in the model.

Every signal the ranker uses — churn, complexity, recency, fan-in — points at the wrong
target on this codebase. XGBoost does not fix this because it is learning from the same
signals. It just learns the misleading correlation more efficiently.

This is not a calibration problem. There is no weight adjustment or threshold tuning that
recovers useful P@K here. The signal is structurally wrong for this type of codebase.

---

## The Pattern Is Predictable

Mature scientific and ML libraries have a structure that breaks activity-based risk signals
systematically:

- Core APIs are touched constantly for maintenance, optimization, and backward-compatibility
  work. They accumulate high churn and high complexity.
- Core APIs rarely regress. The team is expert, tests are dense, review is thorough.
- Bugs live in peripheral code — integrations, utilities, edge-case handlers — that is
  touched infrequently and has low complexity scores.

Any ranker that conflates "touched a lot" with "likely to break" will invert on this pattern.
The more carefully the ranker learns from the repo's history, the more confidently it gets
the wrong answer.

Contrast with Django, a large web framework:

| Repo | Base rate | P@10 |
|---|---|---|
| Django | 47.9% | **1.000** |
| scikit-learn | 21.0% | **0.000** |

On Django, the top 10 functions are all bugs. On scikit-learn, none are. Same ranker,
same signals, opposite results — because the underlying structure of where bugs live is
different.

---

## The Deeper Problem: Different Axes Surface Different Files

This failure points at something more fundamental. The reason churn and complexity
identify the wrong files on scikit-learn is not that these signals are bad — it is that
defect risk and activity risk are measuring different things.

I ran a correlation study across 33 open-source codebases, measuring four dimensions:

- **Defect risk** — fraction of commits to a file that were bug fixes
- **Activity risk** — composite of churn, complexity, recency (the standard hotspot score)
- **Coupling** — how many distinct files this file tends to change alongside
- **Ownership concentration** — how concentrated authorship is on this file

Results:

| Pair | Mean correlation | What this means |
|---|---|---|
| Defect risk × Activity risk | +0.668 | Largely co-measuring, but not identical |
| Defect risk × Coupling | −0.035 | Nearly independent |
| Defect risk × Ownership concentration | −0.016 | Nearly independent |

Activity risk and defect risk are correlated — on average, busy files do tend to have
more defects. But the correlation is not tight enough to treat them as equivalent, and
on repos like scikit-learn the correlation inverts entirely.

Coupling and ownership concentration are nearly independent of defect risk. Files with
many co-change partners are not the same files as high-defect files. Files with concentrated
authorship are not systematically more or less bug-prone. These axes identify genuinely
different code.

This matters for what a risk tool should show you. A ranking that only surfaces activity
risk is leaving coupling risk and ownership risk entirely invisible — and, on mature stable
codebases, it may be actively pointing you at the wrong place.

---

## What the Right Evaluation Looks Like

**Report P@K alongside ρ.** Correlation across all files can be positive while the top
of the ranking is worthless. P@10 and P@20 measure the thing engineers actually act on.

**Know your base rate.** P@10 = 0.100 sounds bad until you know the base rate is 0.050.
P@10 = 0.200 sounds good until you know the base rate is 0.210 (random). P@K only means
something relative to what random selection would produce.

**Flag score collapse.** If a ranker produces fewer than 10 distinct values across hundreds
of functions, its top bucket is likely to be unreliable. Three distinct values — as
happened on scikit-learn — is a strong warning sign that the signal has no discriminating
power at the top.

**When P@K < base rate, suppress.** Showing a confidently wrong ranking is worse than
showing nothing. A tool that surfaces 10 files and zero are defective trains engineers to
ignore it. Detecting when the ranker has inverted and suppressing its output on those repos
is the right engineering response.

---

## Caveats

- scikit-learn is one codebase. The P@10 = 0 finding is specific to its structure
  (stable core APIs dominating the high-activity bucket). Other mature scientific libraries
  may follow the same pattern; others may not.
- The correlation study used 33 repos, but defect risk × activity risk correlation was
  only available on 12 (those with sufficient defect labeling). The 0.668 figure should
  be treated as a central estimate, not a universal constant.
- "Suppression" as a response to P@K < base rate is a judgment call. The right threshold
  depends on how costly a bad top-10 is relative to no ranking at all.
